Australian Alpine Region With The Ricoh GRiiix

📅 Sep 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

And so it is we come to the end of another Australian snow season, and what a season it was. For all intents and purposes, this was one of the worst snow seasons on record in terms of snow cover. Most resorts are closing well over a month early. Many barely got to open at all, and that’s before a near apocalyptic amount of rain descended down upon the Snowies.

So yeah, not the best skiing, but we were able to get out there for a few days and take advantage of what cover there was before it really started to become more like grass skiing. I don’t think I’ve ever seen it quite so patchy before.

The cloud formations in alpine regions are absolutely fascinating to me.

The Ricoh GRiiix continues to be a really fun little camera. I cannot tell you how refreshing it is not having to bring a bag when you’re heading out. You pop it into your pocket, and that’s it. There was one instance at the top of the chairlift where I stepped into a snowdrift with Ricoh in hand an dropped a good four feet or so into the snow. The Ricoh went down with me, the lens compartment completely stuffed with ice and leading to some interesting hazy images like the one below before I was able to completely clear out the snow. I have heard these cameras hate water, but for now it seems to be working okay.

With snow still on the lens, you get this old-timey almost archival feel to the image.

The Hard Monotone preset in the Ricoh GRiiix yet again working wonders with light, here with Australia’s highest ‘mountain’ somewhere there in the background.

This is the view from the top of the chairlift at Charlotte Pass. It’s always changing according to the weather.

Given the lack of snow, we ended up doing a couple of short walks: one across the summit, one to Spencer’s Creek and another up the hill the start of the Main Range track. The latter is where things went sideways.

Long story short, I slipped on some black ice, fractured my spine and required a bit of a rescue mission to get out of there. That’s why I’m sitting here writing this in a brace about to go into surgery tomorrow. Not the greatest of ends to our trip, but that’s how it goes. A big thankyou to all the medical, emergency and rescue staff who helped out on the day.

The chalet at Charlotte Pass has been there forever. I love the alpine, historic feel of the place.

Walking down to Spencer’s Creek, again with the Hard Monotone and again with snow on the lens.

Country NSW With The Ricoh GRiiix

📅 Aug 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s official. I have lost my mind. To the surprise of no one, I sold my Fuji XT5 and picked up a Ricoh GRiiix (40mm equivalent). But it’s half the megapixels! It’s a compact! It’s not a serious camera! We will get to that, but what about the XT5?

Saturated tones near sunset driving around town. This is the popular Royal Supra recipe.

Don’t get me wrong, the XT5 is a supremely capable camera. The IQ is great, autofocus is great, it’s portable, plenty of lenses, but I just found it… boring. I’m always drawn to unique cameras, something a bit left of field. In the digital realm, my favourite camera was the quirky Sigma Quattro series. I had so much fun with the DP-2, and a big factor was how portable it was. It truly was, as I put it, medium format in your pocket.

In a similar vein then, the Ricoh is one of the few digital cameras with high IQ that can fit into a pocket... literally. What about the Fuji X100 series? You’re going to need a big pocket. Same for the XE5. The Ricoh, on the other hand, you can take anywhere. It’s tiny. Better still, it blends in. No one is looking at it twice.

View fullsize
View fullsize
View fullsize

Above, you can see images taken with the Hard Monotone (B&W) filter on the Ricoh GRiiix. It gives a very moody, cinematic look.

I would almost argue the popularity of the Ricoh is down to a single photographer: Daido Moriyama. His high contrast street scenes from Japan are pretty well known. There is even an in-built profile if you’re looking to replicate his look. The Ricoh GR is popular for street photography, but less so for landscape, so let’s see what we can do about that.

I must say, from the very first shot I took, there is this strange magical quality to the Ricoh that makes anything, no matter how ordinary, look interesting and cinematic. In a technical sense, I think this is down to highlight priority metering mode and the in-built profiles. The colour science is unique, and highlight metering meters for the highlights, which is what you want shooting digital (unlike film, where you expose for the shadows). It boggles my mind no other manufacturers offer highlight priority metering. Why?

The burning of the wicker man at the Newbridge Winter Festival. Nice way to warm up.

The combination of the sensor and highlight priority metering lead to filmic images like this, which reminds me of Kodak Portra.

Camera profiles, or recipes, are a big deal in the Ricoh community. The preceding Ricoh GRII was lauded for its Positive Film preset, and while the GRIII doesn’t quite have that specific magic, there are plenty of user recipes that are available. I largely stuck to Royal Supra, which is based on positive slide film and gives punchy, saturated colour. I have had Reggie’s Color Negative and Bennett’s profile loaded as well, but I’m still in testing mode with everything. For B&W, I’ve found the Hard Monotone profile especially good. It’s almost like applying a IR or hard red filter. It works wonders on skies.

I should point out these are applied in-camera to JPEGs. I didn’t actually edit the RAW files at all, which is a nice change. That said, half the RAW files didn’t record because I bumped the top joystrick/wheel and it set to JPEG instead of JPEG + RAW. Cest la vie.

But even using the standard profiles there is a filmic quality to the images. The dynamic range is great, sharpness fine. I wouldn’t push above 3200 ISO, but that’s a personal preference. As I’ve explained recently, I’m not so big on sharpness and perfectly clean files these days. I like a bit of grit, of character.

View fullsize
View fullsize

As for the 40mm focal length, the original, wide GR (now in its IV model) is just too wide for me. It’s too close to the angle you’d get from a smartphone. Ideally, I shoot more around 85mm, but I figured this would be a good way to get me out of my comfort zone. There is a telephoto adapter for the GRiiix, but again, it largely makes it unpocketable.

The photos here were taken during a two-day roadtrip around the NSW Central West. We covered around 1000km checking out the smaller towns out in the country and taking our time. As always, good food was a priority. The weather was mostly fine, so it was a good chance to test out how the Ricoh went with big blue Australian skies.

I think every boy of the 1980s had this poster on their wall. How this one found its way into an abandoned shopfront (next to a mannequin of a Native American in headdress) is a mystery.

I loved this super retro scene at Cowra Railway Station. It was like I’d been transported back to my childhood right down to the extension chord.

You do see a lot of quirky things in these smaller country towns, many of which seem to survive on the mining industry alone, with the positives and negatives that brings. It was also interesting to see the canola feeds in bloom, which usually doesn’t happen until a bit later in the year.

I always like to book at least one night in unique accommodation (see former stays in planes, trains, you name it). This time we stayed in a silo. That is, two silos converted into living spaces with a kitchen and longue between them on a working farm. It was a great experience. The owners had really thought of everything. Click here to check it out.

So Ricoh GRiiix downsides? Not too many, actually. A megapixel bump would be welcome, as would be weather-sealing, and dust (I have heard) can be an issue with the retractable lens. The in-camera aspect ratios are also a bit limited. It would be nice to have at least 3:4, or better 4:5 or 6:7. I’m having to crop to 3:4 in post. Why? I just prefer the format, as it’s a little closer to medium format sizing and tends to break away from the ‘this was shot on a phone/compact.’ My ultimate aspect ratio is 1:1, but that would mean scrapping half the sensor size of the Ricoh. Maybe I sense a medium-format 6x6 film camera in the works…

I’ve also heard a lot of people complaining about the battery, but I had no issues. I always shut the camera off when not in use, and the start-up time is super fast. Combine that with snap focus and it’s easy to see why street photographers love this camera so much.

I had seen this now derelict church on Google maps and had to find it. I just love the color and design.

This guy is part of Utes in the Paddock at Condobolin, which is well worth a look if you’re heading past that way.

All in, this is a magical little camera I have ignored for too long. I’m going to take it on the City 2 Surf fun run in Sydney this weekend, which is definitely something I wouldn’t have been able to do with the Fuji XT5 or GFX.

Anyhow, tune in next week when I decide to trade it all in for a Hasselblad X2D.

The Joy Of 35mm Film

📅 Aug 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I’ve talked in the past about my film photography, notably shooting medium format with my Mamiya RB67 and also the Mamiya 7 for a stage there. As for 35mm, I’d always considered it a lesser format. It doesn’t have the image quality of medium format, the depth, and so on. Growing up in the 1980s and 1990s, everyone shot 35mm. Mum shot it. Dad shot it. My grandparents shot it, and subsequently had us sitting around for holiday slide shows all night.

You can get all kings of wild 35mm disposable cameras in Japan. This was a Lomography LomoChrome Turquoise disposable at the famous (and packed) Golden Pavilion in Kyoto. Having a lot of light helped.

Sometime last year, however, I started getting the itch to shoot film again. I suppose a lot of us are looking towards more analog formats in an increasingly digital world, and especially with the prevalence of AI. You want something physical, tangible and real, and that means imperfection. I’ve embraced a bit of this imperfection with my digital work—lowering sharpening and learning to live with slightly noisy or out-of-focus images.

But film is expensive, especially medium format, and I was after something I could blast away frames with, not really thinking about it too much and hoping for that in-the-moment magic handheld film photography offers. So I decided to pick up a 35mm film camera.

Lomography Redscale. Some interesting light here with the Canon AV-1.

What camera? That’s the question. There are near infinite choices here, but I wanted something as manual as possible, and as simple as possible. I had a Canon AE1 for a while, but sold it off because it was simply never being used. It was almost perfect as far as operation went, but I’m an aperture priority guy by nature. I didn’t even know Canon had made an AP version of this camera until I stumbled upon the Canon AV-1.

It's about as manual as you can get. You select the lens aperture and the camera detects shutter speed from 2sec to 1/1000. There’s also a bulb mode and a kind of exposure comp ‘cheat’ button for backlit scenes, which actually does come in useful when you want to overexpose. I picked mine up for around $250 with a Canon FD 50mm f2—kind of midrange lens. Most tend to opt for the optically superior 50 f1.4, but hey, I wasn’t after perfection.

What’s it been like? In a word, fun. It brings the spontaneity back to photography, and god is it nice hearing that shutter and winding on. It’s such a beautiful, mechanical process you simply do not get with digital. There was a bit of trial and error with loading, forcing me back to YouTube once or twice, but I think I’ve got the hang of it now.

The real fun part, though, is choosing film. In recent years there has been a real influx of new and unusual films on the market given the resurgence of film photography. I did a bit of a deep dive and grabbed some of the crazier films I could find hoping, again, for that don’t-know-what-you’re-getting magic.

Try as you might, it’s just impossible to get this look, these tones, from digital. This is Kodak Gold 200.

I’ve been trying out various black-and-white films, including Kentmere and Ilford. This is HP5.

What I’ve found especially crazy is seeing many of the digital photographers I follow who shot film way back in the day (and swore off never using it again), now coming back into the fold and picking it up again. It’s addictive. In a world where everything is now, now, now, gimmie, gimmie, gimmie, waiting for something is a breath of fresh air. And no editing required. That’s another plus: less time in front of a screen. I usually do some minor cropping, a slight colour adjustment, but that’s generally it.

So this is a very strange film from US company Psych Blues. They hand modify each roll to create these random effects, like this alien attack on the Opera House here. This is Psych Blues 5.

This spot in Barangaroo is a real fave of mine, with the Crown building in the background. It matched the feel of the day given the scorching 45 degree temperature.

As for processing, I’m using Irohas Photo Film Lab here in Sydney. It’s a slick production that gets your scans back within the day, often within an hour. The pricing is also very competitive. The guys there are well connected to the Sydney film community, happy to answer any questions and so on. Highly recommended.

I’ve still got six or so rolls here of random films to shoot. Some of them are quite obscure, so let’s see how they go before I decide to get back into medium format again (It’s going to happen).

Kodak Gold.

I have come to love the grain. As they say, grain is good.

Rather strange bokeh from the Canon AV- and 50 f2 combo, but I quite like it.

I really like the tones in this image. There is a temptation to pump up the levels, but I think that would take away from the mood somewhat.

Heading into autumn, I liked the way these trees were catching last light.

International travel seems off the cards at the moment given everything that is happening in the larger world. But as I always say, there is so much to see in Australia. Hopefully we can squeeze in a road trip or two before the year ends.

For Xmas this year we headed up with the fam to a caravan park in South West Rocks, about halfway between Sydney and Brisbane. South West Rocks is the quintessential Aussie coastal town, so I did my best to try and capture some of that breezy summer magic.

I’m a big fan of this shot with its bendy tree, cool morning light cutting across the hill, the jogger, the ibis and the man walking his dog. It’s everything coastal Australia.

The South West Rocks region has a couple of historical drawcards, notably the Smoky Cape lighthouse and the Trial Bay Gaol. I had visited the goal when I was a kid and was pleased to find the government had poured a bunch of money into restoring and improving facilities around it. Yes, you do have to pay an entry fee, but I think it’s a small price to pay for keeping this slice of history open and in good condition.

There’s a rather ominous feeling walking through the cells and structures. It must have been a tough life out here back in those days. I tried to get this feeling of isolation through in my images. For those visiting, be sure to head up the top for a killer view over the ocean.

Back into the NSW countryside. The great thing to returning to a location is finding new roads and new things, new places to explore. All these shots were taken around sunset one afternoon during a hour drive around the Blayney region.

I actually took a very similar image to this with my film camera a few months ago, but the lit-up curtain of rain here is something special.

I don’t know what it is about Carcoar, but whenever I head there for sunset it’s almost always amazing light. This particular afternoon a passing storm meant the sunset lit up these curtains of rain. It was really quite magical, and the light kind of shifted and morphed from there as we drove around the backroads.

It’s always a mad rush when you get good light like this. You run around trying to find some foreground interest, second-guessing and out of breath. I swear the amount of times I’ve calmly stayed in one place during a good sunset I could count on one hand. I suppose you get caught up in the excitement of it all.

I liked the way the sunset turned the windows of this church into golden panels of light.

This is not some exotic volcano or mount but instead the local council gravel pile, as you can see here below…

There’s plenty to see in the region, though it’s notably dominated by wind farms. Apparently, there had been some serious gusts coming through, because one turbine had a snapped blade. There’s also a massive amount of infrastructure that surrounds these turbines, like fencing, security and the actual power poles and assorted cabling required to ferry all this power out—all of which make for interesting subjects with the right kind of light.

A benefit of the Fuji XT5 is its low-light abilities. With five stops of IBIS I could shoot very late, almost into night, handheld, which is something the GFX would have really struggled with. As such, a lot of these shots tend darker than what I normally shoot, but I do find myself leaning into this style more.

Very eerie light here with the storm in the background. The sheep didn’t seem concerned.

Even fairly industrial subjects look good with the right lighting in place.

I’ll definitely be out in this region a few more times this year exploring some of the satellite towns around the region. It’s very centrally placed, so there’s a lot to see… and a lot of good eating to be had. As always, more shots below.

South Pacific Cruising With The Fuji XT5

📅 Jan 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I do enjoy a good photographic challenge, but what opportunities does a cruise ship offer? This wasn’t my first time doing the usual South Pacific run on a cruiseliner, but it was the first time I brought both a film and digital camera.

A South Pacific cruise distilled down to its most base form: sky and ship

My first thought was to get some minimalistic shots of the waterslides against a blue sky with cotton-ball clouds. Alas, no waterslides. Apparently, this ship was built for cold-weather climates. But we did have great weather and no significant swells (thank jebus), so what did I actually shoot? Well, not much, but let’s have a look anyhow.

I generally like to start off by walking around the top deck of the ship, which is where all the action tends to happen. I often think a cruise would been a boon for a proper street photographer given the colourful collection of characters they tend to attract and the gaudy excess that goes along with it. Given my aversion to people in general, though, it’s not really for me. Instead, I focus on shapes and colours, interesting visual patterns.

View fullsize
View fullsize
View fullsize

This particular ship was in no shortage of attractions, with a Flowrider out the back, even indoor skydiving. There was a large gym complex with dodgem cars, pickleball, skating… You name it. It’s quite remarkable what they manage to shoehorn onto these ships these days.

Where possible, I think it’s good (budget allowing) to book a room with a balcony, especially if you have a camera with you. A few times we passed storms in the distance, but given the speed the ship was travelling at it would be easy to miss them if you had to leg it outside and up to the deck for a view. Speaking of which, that is my personal secret to staying fit on a cruise ship: never use the elevators. Trust me, you’ll have legs of steel after a week. And steer clear of the buffet. Nothing good comes of that.

A broody storm brewing through the haze of ship exhaust.

I haven’t put the film in to be developed, but the digital shots came up okay. I think I’m still on the fence about the XT5. It’s such a capable camera, but I’m not sure if it inspires in the same way the GFX did. I think maybe I just need to spend more time with it. As I’ve said before, and especially when we were in port, the lighter weight came in very handy. There is no way I would have been lugging around the GFX and a film camera.

I quite liked the window reflections in the main atrium section of the ship.

The aforementioned indoor skydiving on board, AKA iFly. This instructor clearly had a few hours under his belt.

Do I recommend cruising? Sure, it’s a great way to travel. You unpack once and don’t have to worry about public transport or which train to catch, about where to dine or what to do. There’s always something going on. Is it a little tacky, sure? But you kind of have to go along with it. It is what you make it, as they say, and certainly some locations, like Mystery Island, are as beautiful as ever. Scroll down for more images.

I never miss up a chance to shoot the Opera House. We actually arrived in Circular Quay the night before to avoid a large low-pressure system coming up the coast. It was magic waking up to this view out your window.








ICM Photography Part III

📅 Nov 13, 2025  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

It’s been quite a while (years, in fact) since I put up a ICM (Intentional Camera Movement) post. I still get around to shooting ICM subjects in my native Sydney, but there have been a few subjects that have eluded me over the years. Let’s get into it.

The Museum of Contemporary Art. The jacarandas in the foreground really make it.

This is the Museum of Contemporary Art as seen from the Cahill Walk. I have had this image in my mind for quite a while, but I needed to wait until jacaranda season to make it work. During this time, you’ll find pops of purple all over Sydney thanks to these trees, and these ones in particular just happen to sit below the walkway and perfectly work as foreground to the museum and Sydney Harbour Bridge beyond. For the tourists among you, the Cahill Walk is the perfect way to get away from the crowds at Circular Quay and get a better vantage point of the harbour. Simply use the elevator at the end of the Circular Quay.

The warm colour of the sandstone works well with the purple of the jacarandas, I think. There’s also a nice sense of movement in the image. And who can argue with something as iconic as the Harbour Bridge in the background. Overall, it’s become one of my favourite ICM images to date. Check out this image to see how this vantage point looks without the movement.

Bondi Icebergs Ocean Pool. There are few places in Sydney as iconic, but this image has eluded me time and time again.

The equally famous Icebergs ocean pool at Bondi Beach is a subject I must have tried to shoot three times using ICM, always looking ridiculous shaking my camera ’round up top at the viewing spot. I just could never get it to quite work, so this time I really thought about how I would frame the composition. I wanted the blues of the pool to be very punchy surrounded by the wash of the ocean. The lifeguard in the bottom right is also a neat touch.

Afterwards, my son and I walked down and went for a swim. I’ve lived in Sydney almost all my life but have never actually swum in this pool. There’s something about it—doing laps in the sun (or storm, as it was this day), Bondi beach busy and lively. I know it’s very touristy, but if you’re in Sydney I’d highly recommended dropping in for a quick dip. Being a public pool, entry is reasonably cheap.

Sydney Town Hall. Another elusive location.

Recently, I discussed trading in my Fujifilm GFX50R for the Fujifilm XT5. These next ICM images were taken with that camera. What I have noticed is you have to be a bit more careful with the XT5 files, especially given the post-production abuse I level upon them making these images. Go too hard and banding and artefacts start to become visible. That said, the results are still great, plus it’s so much easier to get around the city with a smaller camera.

I’ve tried many different compositions of Town Hall in the centre of Sydney but could never really get it working the way I wanted. Recently, I tried again and think I got it. There’s a jacaranda tree and something undetermined in the bottom right, the movement enough but not overboard and the colours pleasing. It’s fairly heavy (dark) tonally in the bottom corner, but I kind of like that, as if someone really slathered on the paint there.

Saint Mary’s Cathedral. Quite a departure from my regular ICM style.

While I was out, I also swung by Saint Mary’s Cathedral on the other side of the city—another icon. I had also tried to shoot this a few times with no success and came to it a bit more prepared. There’s a long water feature across from the cathedral that is great for reelections, so I used this to eliminate some of the busy foreground. As always, I was using shutter speeds between half a second to two seconds, really trying everything terms of moving the camera with the ND filter in place (rather, holding it in front of the lens given I don’t have an adapter yet).

During processing, it looked good but wasn’t quite working until I dropped it into Silver Efex. The result was an extremely dark, almost haunting image that looked like washy watercolour or charcoal. I debated if it was too far or maybe too different from my other ICM images, but ultimately I’ve decided to keep it in, as I quite like the look. Although it looks extremely inky, there is detail in there at 100%.

All this to say I think I’m running out of subjects in Sydney for ICM. If you have any ideas, please let me know and I’ll do my best to accommodate.

Japan With The Fuji XT5

📅 Oct 27, 2025  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

And so it was we headed to Japan this year for a family holiday. As these things go, photography is never a priority on these kind of trips, but I did manage to sneak in a few shots… though in a new direction.

This is my favourite image. It was taken at TeamLab Borderless in Tokyo, a kind of light and projection installation. This little girl was wearing a bright orange dress and sliding down one of the structures (to the dismay of a nearby security guard), but the lighting and shutter speed give it a painterly, abstract feel.

My wife and I last visited Japan twenty years ago (before kids). A lot has changed, yet a lot has also stayed the same. For one, the amount of tourists has increased significantly. Almost everyone I spoke to this year seemed like they were planning on going or had been to Japan. It really is the place to be, as evidenced how busy it is over there at the moment. And while it’s still ‘the future’ in many ways, there is also this strange dichotomy in which Japan is also stuck back in the ’80s—paper ticketing, cash and fax machines. I spoke to a local in Osaka who told me with an ageing population there is a mantra of ‘if it ain’t broke, don’t fix it’ and an unwillingness to change, especially in business. This was evidenced by major beer supplier Asahi suffering a cyber attack while we were there and having to resort to paper and fax again to get their product out. It really is a country caught in a strange tug-of-war between old and new.

View fullsize

The above images taken out of the window on the flight to and from Japan.

We did the usual run between Tokyo, Osaka and Kyoto. Apart from eating the country out of Kobe and wagyu beef, the kids loved it. There’s plenty to do. A lot of photographers would thumb their nose at visiting and taking images at popular tourist spots, but I always see it as a challenge. I was quite excited for TeamLab Borderless in Tokyo, maybe the most popular tourist attraction there, and also the golden pavilion in Kyoto, again as especially popular place.

More light-projection fun at TeamLab Borderless. I think the silhouette adds a point of difference.

I knew from my last time in Japan it is not an easy place to photograph. While minimalism seems ingrained in Japanese culture, the cities themselves are very maximalist. There is so much going on and so much visual clutter I find it hard to simply walk and find good compositions. What I ended up with then was a very different set of images that micro-focused on things like plays of light and very subtle compositions I hope sum up the experience there. I actually returned very happy with these images, some of which suffer technically but I think have genuine character and life. It was certainly a different approach.

Kinkakuji, or the Golden Pavilion, is one of the most popular tourist destinations in Japan. So, how do you take a unique shot of something that’s been photographed endlessly? Shoot through the water bottle you’re holding with a red umbrella in the foreground. Whether it works it up to interpretation.

I’ve called this series Enter Hikari, the latter being Japanese for ‘light.’ Yes, I’m a big Metallica and Enter Shakari fan from way back, but I think it encapsulates what I’m trying to get across here—that light is the focus of my photography these days in all its many and varied forms. Moving forward, I think I’m going to push for this even more.

A better example of an image I might normally take…

…versus what I ended up with. This is light falling on a wall at a cafe in Kyoto with, my wife assures me, very good coffee, which says a lot coming from an Australian.

Regular readers might also notice the shift from the Fuji GFX50R to the Fuji XT5. I’ve been thinking about this a while and decided ultimately I needed a lighter, more compact set-up for travelling. I have loved using the GFX, but combined with the 45-100mm lens it is quite heavy. I had been considering the XE5, but given the price it made more practical sense to go with the XT5 for its weather sealing and dual card slots.

So how is the IQ difference between the GFX and the XT5? During this trip I used what amounts to a kit zoom lens with the XT5. Although there is only 10 megapixels of difference between the two cameras, the physical size of the GFX sensor is much larger, so you will aways get more micro detail and more latitude in the files, but I was very impressed with the how the XT5 held up. I imagine most people looking at my images on the web would not be able to tell the difference between the two systems, but you do have to be mindful of blowing highlights and keeping in the back of your mind that the XT5 files aren’t quite as malleable as those from the GFX.

A significant amount of these images were actually taken in the various accommodation we stayed at as opposed to out and about. The textures and light gave this flower display, again, a painterly feel.

But my god is it nice having a light camera again. It almost feels like a toy in the hand. The autofocus is also far, far superior and quicker than the GFX, and the built-in IBIS blew me away. I took one shot handheld at 2sec and was surprised it was completely sharp all the way through. There was also a point where I accidentally bumped the ISO dial and shot a number of images over 12,000 ISO. Using Denoise in Adobe Camera Raw, I was shocked these images became usable.

Where to next then? We have a couple of family trips planned next year. I’ve also been shooting a lot of 35mm film, which has been fun, and I’m continuing with my Sydney ICM (Intentional Camera Movement) series. I’ll have a post on that soon. Until then, enjoy more images below or see the full gallery here.
